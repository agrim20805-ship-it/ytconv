What is copyright, and how do Creative Commons licences change it?

Short answer

What copyright gives an owner

  • Owners have exclusive rights to control how others use their work, including the right to reproduce it in any format, publish it, perform or communicate it, and adapt it University of South Australia 00:26 University of South Australia 00:30.
  • Using someone's work, or a substantial part of it, in these ways without permission is infringing copyright University of South Australia 00:43.
  • Owners also have moral rights: using someone's work in an assignment without acknowledging them is plagiarism, even if the work has already been shared online University of South Australia 00:51 University of South Australia 00:56.
  • If you take a photo on your phone, you own it and have the right to exploit it for your own commercial benefit Wikimedia UK 00:21.
  • You must get someone's permission before sharing or reusing their work, even when it is posted online Matt P. Wolf 00:42.

Where copyright came from

  • Britain has had copyright laws since the 1600s, when printing companies wanted to stop other printers selling copies of their books Wikimedia UK 00:28.
  • The 1709 Statute of Anne, often seen as the first real copyright law, gave publishers rights for a fixed period: 14 years, renewable for a similar term Wikimedia UK 00:52 Wikimedia UK 01:03.
  • The US Constitution took the same 14-year term, renewable once, in 1787 Wikimedia UK 01:09.
  • The 1886 Berne Convention said authors do not have to assert copyright for it to apply, and extended the term to 50 years after the author's death Wikimedia UK 01:19.
  • Today, in most of the world, copyright lasts for the author's life plus 50 or 70 years; it usually ends with the calendar year, so January 1st is celebrated as Public Domain Day Wikimedia UK 01:41 Wikimedia UK 01:47.
  • The video dates the WTO's TRIPS agreement to 1985 Wikimedia UK 01:30; the agreement was signed in 1994 (not from the videos).

What a student may copy

  • Students may copy a reasonable portion of someone else's work for research and study University of South Australia 01:11.
  • As a general rule that means up to 10% of a book, even online, a whole article, or an image - as long as you acknowledge your sources University of South Australia 01:22 University of South Australia 01:27.
  • Using someone's work without permission outside your studies may be breaking the law University of South Australia 01:44.
  • Sharing lecture notes, readings, past exam papers or journal articles on unauthorised sharing sites breaches copyright and the student code of conduct University of South Australia 02:16.

How Creative Commons licences work

  • A licence refines your copyright and streamlines how you give permission Matt P. Wolf 01:03.
  • Licences are made of four rules, each with its own symbol: attribution (credit the creator) Matt P. Wolf 01:47, non-commercial (no one else may make money from the work) Matt P. Wolf 01:57, no derivatives (no changes without asking) Matt P. Wolf 02:12, and share alike (new works must carry the same licence) Matt P. Wolf 02:28.
  • Attribution is in every licence; the other three are optional, which makes six combinations Matt P. Wolf 02:57 Matt P. Wolf 03:03.
  • The Attribution licence gives re-users the most freedom and Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivatives the least Matt P. Wolf 03:11.
  • Like normal copyright, a Creative Commons licence keeps the author's right to be identified as the creator Wikimedia UK 02:23. Wikimedia's own content is shared under CC BY-SA 4.0 Wikimedia UK 02:13.
  • Creative Commons work can be found with the search filter on the Creative Commons website, Jamendo for music and Flickr for images Matt P. Wolf 04:09 Matt P. Wolf 04:17.

Where they agree

  • By default you need the owner's permission to reuse a work University of South Australia 00:43 Matt P. Wolf 00:42.
  • Acknowledging the creator matters, under copyright and under Creative Commons University of South Australia 00:56 Wikimedia UK 02:23 Matt P. Wolf 02:43.

Where they differ

  • Permission. Matt P. Wolf says you must always get permission before sharing or reusing someone's work Matt P. Wolf 00:42, while the University of South Australia says students may copy a reasonable portion for research and study University of South Australia 01:11. They are talking about different situations: the university video is about study, the Creative Commons video about sharing in general.
  • What copyright is for. The University of South Australia presents copyright as something that protects everyone, including you University of South Australia 02:31, and asks students to comply with it University of South Australia 02:41. Wikimedia UK presents open licences as a response to how widespread copyright has become Wikimedia UK 01:57 and wants more content shared freely Wikimedia UK 03:23.
  • Social media. Only Wikimedia UK says that uploading to Facebook, Twitter or Instagram gives those companies the right to reuse your content without asking or crediting you Wikimedia UK 03:00.
